Overview

Facial affect perception tasks typically assume identical stimuli are equally difficult for all participants, an assumption violated by individual differences in perceptual sensitivity. We propose PAQ, a lightweight query mechanism that estimates each participant's Just Noticeable Difference (JND) along the facial affect spectrum, and use it to re-express stimulus distances in perceptual rather than physical units. Validated on a Two-Alternative Forced-Choice task using metacognitive difficulty judgments and response-time variance, PAQ calibration significantly equalizes perceived task difficulty at the individual level, and it outperforms both non-calibrated and population-level Weibull baselines. These results establish PAQ as a practical instrument for individualized perceptual calibration in facial affect recognition.

The 2AFC task, and why a fixed interval isn't fair

A common way to study facial emotion perception is a two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC) task: participants view a pair of faces drawn from a facial affect spectrum and judge whether they express the same emotion at the same intensity — yes or no — while both their choice and response time are recorded.

These stimulus pairs are typically built from a fixed physical interval, Δx, applied identically to every participant. But perceptual sensitivity is not uniform: the same Δx that is barely noticeable to one participant sits right at another's threshold, and is unmistakably obvious to a third.

This directly confounds downstream science. In depression research, patients are thought to struggle more with happy faces than sad ones, measured by response time. But if our happy-face stimuli are simply harder to tell apart to begin with, regardless of who's looking, then every participant would be slower there — and we'd mistake that stimulus artifact for a clinical symptom.

Our goal in this paper is: how to mitigate such miscalibration in facial-recognition 2AFC tasks.

Observation: JNDs are not constant across the population

Before proposing a fix, we asked how much perceptual sensitivity actually varies. We collected each participant's just-noticeable difference (JND) against reference faces from four demographic groups. Mean 1-JND ranges from 9.9 to 14.2 frames across groups — close to a 50% spread in threshold size — and substantial individual variation exists within every group as well.

Method

A perceptual adjustment query (PAQ) asks a participant to move a slider along a continuous stimulus path until the displayed face first looks affectively different from a fixed reference face. Because the slider traverses the whole path, one response implicitly compares the reference against every point along the way, so a single query yields a dense, person-specific estimate of that participant's 1-JND at that reference point — without requiring a full staircase or repeated binary trials. We realize this in three steps.

  1. Affect spectrum construction. Starting from a neutral face, we prompt a video-generation vision-language model to produce a slow, smooth transition to a target expression (happy or sad), preserving identity throughout. From the resulting video, 100 sequential frames are extracted to form a monotonically ordered, identity-preserved facial path.
  2. Local JND calibration. A reference face is shown at a fixed but undisclosed position on this path. Participants move a continuous slider until the comparison face first appears affectively different, and the stopping distance defines their JND at that reference point — recovered from a single slider pull.
  3. Behavioral 2AFC task validation. Each participant's individual JNDs are used to place the two faces in a downstream same/different comparison exactly one JND apart, so every participant is judging a pair at the boundary of their own discriminability. We compare calibrated and non-calibrated 2AFC tasks across two rounds of user studies and test the significance of the calibration.

Generating a stimulus path that is simultaneously continuous, monotonic, identity-preserving, and artifact-free is the main technical challenge. The paper compares AU-based morphing, GAN-based latent traversal, diffusion-based interpolation, and direct multimodal-LLM image prompting against the VLM video-morphing approach used here, and finds that only the latter satisfies all criteria.

Results

Across 124 participants recruited on Prolific, PAQ calibration was compared against both a non-calibrated baseline (fixed physical inter-stimulus distance) and a population-level Weibull calibration. Two behavioral measures were used to test whether calibration equalized perceived task difficulty: binary metacognitive judgments of difficulty and response-time variance decomposition.

PAQ calibration significantly increased the proportion of trials perceived as equally difficult, both relative to the non-calibrated baseline (one-sided exact McNemar test, p = 0.0004) and relative to population-level calibration (p = 0.0014). It also reduced mean response time by roughly 47% and cut between-subject response-time variance by 79–85% relative to the two comparison conditions.

Limitations

Because participants complete PAQ calibration before the post-PAQ 2AFC task, they become more familiar with the stimuli, which the authors note as a possible ordering confound; a suggested remedy is to run calibration before both the baseline and PAQ-calibrated tasks. The framework also assumes each participant's perceptual function is monotone and well-approximated by a Weibull CDF, and its generalization to clinical populations (e.g., depression, autism spectrum disorder), other affective dimensions (arousal, dominance), and cross-cultural settings remains to be demonstrated.
